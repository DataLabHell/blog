Science has a context problem. Faraday makes it machine-readable — so AI agents can retrieve authoritative constants, spectra, thermochemistry, identifiers, and line lists, then reason from them with provenance instead of speculation.

github.com/DataLabHell/faraday

The bottleneck for AI in science is often not reasoning. It is context. The constants, spectra, thermochemistry, molecular identifiers, and line lists needed to answer quantitative questions already exist — but they are scattered across legacy databases with different portals, identifiers, units, and file formats. Faraday gives AI systems access to that authoritative scientific context through one typed, transparent interface.

What this page is. A short tour of why Faraday exists and how it works, followed by three investigations an agent ran end-to-end — a fusion energy ledger, a methane flame's spectrum, and a molecule-by-molecule comparison of CO₂ and CH₄ — each drawing its data from Faraday alone and shipping downloadable data, scripts where present, and provenance notes. Every headline figure below traces to a specific Faraday tool call.

01The scientific context problem

Six bodies of reference data are widely used across the physical sciences. CODATA[3] provides the internationally recommended values of the fundamental constants. HITRAN[2] gives per-transition molecular line parameters for radiative-transfer work. The NIST Chemistry WebBook[4] supplies Shomate thermochemistry, vapour pressures, and measured spectra. The NIST Atomic Spectra Database[7] holds observed lines and energy levels for a wide range of elements and ions. PubChem[6] carries structures, identifiers, and safety data for on the order of a hundred million compounds. MassBank of North America[8] archives experimental mass spectra. Each is the product of decades of careful, curated measurement, and each is independently maintained and widely cited.

They simply do not speak to one another. Every source has its own portal, its own query grammar, its own identifier scheme — a HITRAN molecule number here, a CAS registry number there, a NIST species ID somewhere else — and its own file format: 160-character HITRAN records, JCAMP-DX spectral files, blocks of Shomate coefficients, CODATA constant keys. Units collide too: a band centre is quoted in wavenumbers by one source, microns by another, electronvolts by a third. A scientist has learned how to navigate this fragmentation. An AI agent sees disconnected systems. Faraday closes that gap by turning fragmented reference data into shared scientific context.

02Error-prone, because the infrastructure assumes a human

These interfaces were built for a person at a keyboard. You copy a constant and paste it into a script. You transcribe sixteen Shomate coefficients from a table. You convert 667 cm⁻¹ to 14.98 µm by hand. You match an isotopologue index in HITRAN to an isotope peak in a mass spectrum. Every one of those hops is a silent opportunity for error — a transposed digit, a dropped factor of ten, the wrong isotopologue — and none of them is the science. The databases are carefully maintained; the integration layer is often a person doing it by hand, and people mistype.

This is a quiet tax on legacy scientific infrastructure: often the data is not missing, but turning it into an answer is manual, frequently unaudited, and hard to retrace later. The spreadsheet that produced last year's figure may be gone; the constant someone pasted in, a version nobody recorded.

03An API wants precision; a question starts vague

A REST endpoint cannot answer “what's in air?” It answers molecule=2, isotopologue=1, numin=667, numax=672. To get there you must already know that the species of interest is CO₂, that CO₂ is HITRAN molecule 2, that its principal isotopologue is index 1, and that the 15-micron greenhouse feature is the ν₂ bending fundamental near 667 cm⁻¹. The distance between a scientific question and a well-formed query is the expertise that makes the data hard to reach in the first place.

An agent closes that distance. Ask it about the infrared opacity of air and it does what a spectroscopist would: it decomposes “air” into N₂, O₂, Ar, CO₂, H₂O, CH₄…, recognises that the radiatively active species are the polar and asymmetric ones, resolves each to its HITRAN identifier, picks the relevant bands and windows, retrieves the lines, and reassembles them into an answer — pulling the constants it needs from CODATA and the molecular masses from PubChem along the way. The vague question survives contact with the precise interface, because the agent supplies the missing translation.

The translation, concretely
“How hot is a methane flame, and why is it blue then orange?” becomes: resolve CH₄, CO₂, H₂O and N₂ in the WebBook → pull their Shomate enthalpies → solve an adiabatic flame balance → resolve Na, K, Li in the ASD → get their resonance lines and energy levels → weight them by a Boltzmann factor at the flame temperature → fetch CO₂/H₂O bands from HITRAN for the infrared. One sentence in; a dozen typed, precise tool calls underneath.

04How Faraday works

The Model Context Protocol (MCP)[1] is a thin, typed contract between an agent and a tool server. Faraday advertises 15 tools across the six sources; the agent calls them by name with structured arguments and receives structured data back — no agent-side scraping, no agent-side HTML parsing, no scripting against six different APIs. Faraday owns the provider-specific parsing where an upstream still exposes legacy HTML or CGI forms. Two conventions make it ergonomic. First, a search → get pattern: resolve a human name to a stable identifier (pubchem_search("caffeine") → a CID), then fetch by that identifier. Second, uniform returns: the server normalises each upstream's idiosyncratic format — JCAMP-DX, HITRAN records, Shomate blocks — into clean, typed JSON, so a wavenumber is always a number and a line list is always a table.

The whole stack is introspectable. An agent can list the available tools, read each one's schema, and plan a multi-source query without anything hard-coded in advance. The diagram below traces one question from the top of the stack to the upstream databases and back.

05Self-discovery, not pre-programming

Because the tool list and every schema are introspectable, the agent is not a fixed script bolted onto known endpoints. It discovers what the server offers, what each tool returns, and — the part that matters most for trust — what data is actually available for a given query. Availability is something you learn at runtime, not assume.

A real example from the flame investigation: asked for the heat of combustion of methanol, the agent called nist_get_thermo, found that the WebBook has no gas-phase Shomate table for it, and rather than fabricate a value, pivoted to methane — the literal Bunsen-burner gas — whose coefficients are present, and recorded the gap as a finding. Self-discovery turns a missing dataset from a silent, answer-corrupting failure into an explicit, logged decision. The same mechanism lets the agent notice when a source is intermittently unreachable and say so, instead of quietly returning less than it claims.

06Transparent and auditable by construction

In these investigations, every scientific value the agent uses arrives through a named call with explicit arguments, so the entire derivation is a log you can replay. The investigations ship the available derived data — raw upstream data regenerates on demand from the logged calls, plus analysis or validation scripts where those scripts are checked in. The artifact layout is not identical across all three case studies, so provenance is described per article rather than as a single universal raw-JSON contract. When a result disagrees with the prose, the prose is corrected and the correction is recorded — not hidden.

The constants get a second, independent check. Faraday's CODATA values are compared against scipy.constants, which carries the same internationally adopted CODATA 2022 set, and they agree numerically within the precision checked by the scripts. The point is not that an agent can compute; it is that the agent hands you the number and the receipt: which database, which call, which arguments, cross-checked against an independent implementation. That is the difference between an answer you trust because it sounds right and one you trust because you can re-run it.

07Recalled vs. retrieved

A fair question from anyone who works with these databases: a frontier model already “knows” the speed of light — what does fetching it add? It depends entirely on the kind of number. Memory and retrieval fail differently: memory fails as a silently plausible value, retrieval fails as a visible error. In scientific work those costs are not symmetric.

What an agent needsFrom model memoryThrough Faraday
SI-defined and famous constants (h, c, kB, NA) Reliable — exact by definition since the 2019 SI redefinition, heavily represented in training data. The same digits, plus uncertainty, unit, and the CODATA adjustment cycle pinned in the response. Grounding adds provenance here, not correction.
Curated single facts (boiling points, Antoine validity ranges, GHS classes) Usually close, but editions and conditions blur — which pressure, which table revision, which source. The NIST WebBook or PubChem record itself, with compound ID and table context, quotable as retrieved.
Dense numerical tables (HITRAN line intensities, ASD transition probabilities, Shomate coefficients) Not reliably recallable: thousands of six-plus-digit parameters per molecule. Plausible-looking confabulation is the failure mode. The exact records — e.g. the 90 CO₂ ν₂ lines (651–682 cm⁻¹) behind the Greenhouse analysis, intensities spanning four orders of magnitude, retrieved live via hitran_get_lines and summarised in its appendix; the raw line list regenerates on demand (see the Data availability notice).
Versioned and post-cutoff data (HITRAN2024 updates, new CODATA cycles) Frozen at the training cutoff; adjacent editions blend together. A live fetch that names its edition in the payload (database_edition: HITRAN2024).

It is also testable: the available retrieved tables ship beside the articles, and raw line lists regenerate on demand via the documented tool calls, so you can ask any model for the same records from memory and diff the answer against what Faraday returns.

What the “Status” column means — and where it needs work

Every tool fetches from its upstream service at call time; there is no local mirror, so a tool is only as available as the database behind it. Requests are spaced by a polite per-host rate limit (PubChem up to 5 per second, the others 1 per second), cached in memory for one hour, and routed through a robots.txt-aware client. The column reports what we observe, not a promise:

  • live — the upstream offers a stable, documented interface (a static file, or a versioned REST/HTTP API). Within the rate limit and cache, calls succeed reliably. Covers CODATA constants, PubChem, NIST WebBook thermochemistry, and HITRANonline.
  • oscillates — the NIST Atomic Spectra Database. Its tools drive ASD’s legacy CGI forms (lines1.pl, energy1.pl) and parse tab-delimited text. Two things make it flap between reachable and not: under load NIST rate-limits and sometimes returns an HTML error page instead of data, and the CGI field names have been renamed upstream before (e.g. low_wl → low_w), which silently breaks requests until the form mapping is updated. robots.txt is honoured here; transient robots 5xx/429 responses disallow the current request but are not cached for the full robots window.
  • live / by-ID — the spectra tools (NIST WebBook JCAMP-DX, MoNA mass spectra). Retrieval is reliable once you hold the exact record ID — a WebBook compound ID or a MoNA accession. There is no fuzzy spectrum search, so discovery, not delivery, is the limit: you resolve an ID first (via nist_search_compound or by browsing MoNA), then fetch.
Where stability would improve
  • ASD scraping is brittle. It rides an undocumented CGI form and an HTML/TSV layout NIST can change without notice. Hardening: detect the error-page response and retry with backoff, validate the returned column schema before parsing, and auto-discover the form fields instead of hard-coding them.
  • No spectrum discovery. “by-ID” retrieval should sit behind a name→ID resolver, so a caller can ask for a spectrum by molecule rather than needing the accession up front.
  • The cache is in-memory only. The one-hour response cache is lost on restart and is not shared across processes; a persistent or shared cache would carry the oscillating sources across runs and absorb most transient outages.
  • No cross-process circuit breaker. Transient 429, 5xx, and network-layer request failures are retried with bounded backoff, but outage state is not shared across processes and failing hosts are not temporarily suppressed. A per-host circuit breaker would make repeated “oscillates” episodes quieter for callers.

09Three investigations

To show the gap between reachable data and usable answers, the agent ran three investigations end to end. Each begins with a plain question, draws every number from Faraday alone, and ships an openly documented pipeline with downloadable data and full appendices. They span three corners of the physical sciences — a star's energy source, a candle flame, and the planet's heat balance — and together they exercise all six databases.

Read the difference
Each report opens from a single question and ends with the same thing a referee would want: every figure traceable to a tool call, the available derived data downloadable beside the article (raw regenerates on demand), available scripts or validation notes, and an honest list of what failed. That auditability — more than the prose — is the point.
