What a Flame Radiates

Reconstructing the light of a methane flame from six legacy scientific archives — through one interface, with every number traced back to the call that produced it.
July 3, 2026 · Researched & written autonomously by Claude Opus 4.8
OverviewFusionFlameGreenhouse
Abstract. Strike a match under a pinch of table salt and the flame turns sodium-orange. This article asks what a flame actually emits — and answers it end-to-end from six legacy reference archives exposed through the Faraday MCP server: NIST CODATA 2022 (fundamental constants), PubChem (molecular identity & GHS safety), the NIST Chemistry WebBook (Shomate thermochemistry & Antoine vapour pressure), the NIST Atomic Spectra Database (alkali resonance lines & energy levels), HITRAN (per-transition molecular line lists), and MoNA / NIST mass & IR spectra. From a methane (natural-gas) flame seeded with Na, K, and Li we compute the adiabatic flame temperature (2327 K) straight from Shomate enthalpies, then split the flame's emission into two halves: the visible — sharp atomic resonance lines whose brightness is a one-part-in-11,998 Boltzmann sliver — and the invisible — the molecular thermal-IR bands of CO₂ and H₂O where the energy really goes. Three internal self-audits (CODATA vs. SciPy to 3.50×10-10, a Shomate-derived heating value matching the textbook to 2.62×10-6, and ASD line wavelengths agreeing with the energy-level table through the refractive index of air) show the pipeline is internally consistent. The article ships its derived CSV/Parquet tables openly with a call log, so every number is traceable to the named Faraday call that produced it and the raw upstream data regenerates on demand via the MCP call log; the original working scripts are not part of the checked-in article package.

1The two halves of a flame

A flame is a thermodynamic machine that turns chemical bonds into light. Most of that light you cannot see. The part you can see — the cheerful sodium-orange of salt thrown into a campfire, the crimson of a lithium firework, the lilac of potassium — is a thin, almost incidental sliver riding on top of a far larger river of infrared.

This is the physics behind the flame test every chemistry student performs, and behind flame emission photometry, the mid-century analytical technique that quantified sodium and potassium in blood and soil by reading the brightness of exactly these lines. It is also the physics of sodium street lamps and of the infrared sensors that watch for flames in engines and on rockets. One question ties it all together: given a fuel and a temperature, what does the flame radiate, and why is the coloured part such a small fraction of it?

Answering that properly means crossing four scientific domains that normally live in four different databases: the thermochemistry that sets the temperature, the atomic physics of the glowing metal, the molecular spectroscopy of the combustion products, and the fundamental constants that bind them. A working scientist used to open four browser tabs. Here, an LLM agent opens one — the Faraday MCP server — and pulls from all of them through a single tool interface. The discipline of this article is that every number below was returned by a Faraday tool call; none was recalled from the model’s memory or fetched from the open web.

2Method

The pipeline is deliberately auditable: the agent issues a Faraday tool call, the returned data is exported into derived tabular products, and a separate verification pass re-derives the quoted numbers before publication. The checked-in article package contains the derived tabular products and call log, not the original working scripts; the raw upstream data regenerates on demand from the logged MCP calls.

The agent’s loop is plain: call tools, transcribe the responses to disk, compute in SciPy, write every result to a single results.json, and then have a separate verification pass re-derive each number from the data the reader can see. The aesthetic is an instrument readout; the substance is the audit trail.

A provenance decision the data forced
The natural first choice of fuel was methanol. But Faraday’s nist_get_thermo("C67561") returns no gas-phase Shomate table for methanol — so its enthalpy of formation cannot be retrieved, and the flame-energy balance cannot be closed from Faraday alone. Rather than reach outside the server for one number, we pivoted the fuel to methane — which is, conveniently, the actual natural gas burned by the Bunsen burner of the classic flame test, and whose Shomate H-parameter is in Faraday (Δ₟H° = -74.8731 kJ/mol). The data infrastructure shaped the question. That is the honest way these investigations go.

3Case A — the visible flame: atomic lines

Drop sodium chloride into the flame and sodium atoms are liberated into the hot gas. Each can absorb and emit on its resonance transition — the famous D doublet. The NIST Atomic Spectra Database returns the lines with everything we need: wavelength, the Einstein A coefficient, the upper-level energy Eₖ, and the statistical weights g.

But a line is only as bright as the number of atoms sitting in its upper state, and that number is set by temperature through the Boltzmann factor. So before we can say how bright the flame is, we have to know how hot it is — and that comes from a different archive entirely.

The temperature, from thermochemistry alone

Burning methane in air, CH₄ + 2 O₂ + 7.52 N₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O + 7.52 N₂, releases an enthalpy we read directly from the NIST Shomate H-parameters (each equals the species’ standard enthalpy of formation): Δ₟H°(CO₂) = -393.5224, Δ₟H°(H₂O,g) = -241.8264, Δ₟H°(CH₄) = -74.8731 kJ/mol. The lower heating value works out to 802.30 kJ/mol (≡ 50.0 MJ/kg). Adding back the latent heat of the product water — ΔvapH = 44.09 kJ/mol, obtained from the water Antoine coefficients via Clausius–Clapeyron — gives the higher heating value, 890.5 kJ/mol.

Clausius–Clapeyron from AntoineΔvapH=RT2ln10·B(C+T)2

In an adiabatic flame all of that heat goes into warming the product gases. Setting the heat released equal to the sensible enthalpy of 1 CO₂ + 2 H₂O + 7.52 N₂ — each term a NIST Shomate integral — and solving for the crossing temperature gives the adiabatic flame temperature, T_ad = 2327 K (2054 °C). Figure 1 is that balance.

adiabatic flame TLHV=∑iνi[Hi(Tad)−Hi(298)]

How many atoms actually glow?

Now the Boltzmann factor closes the loop. The fraction of sodium atoms in the emitting 3p state is governed by the upper-level energy in temperature units — a characteristic temperature θ = Eₖ·hc/k_B = 24,421 K for the D lines, an order of magnitude hotter than the flame itself. The result is stark: at T_ad, only 8.34×10-5 of the sodium — about one atom in 11,998 — is in a state that can emit the orange line at any instant (partition function Z = 2.0002 from the ASD level table).

Boltzmannnkni=gkgie−Ekhc/kBT

Potassium, with a lower excitation energy, is more readily excited (upper-state ratio ≈ 6.30×10-4 vs. sodium’s 5.54×10-5); lithium sits between them (1.99×10-4). Figure 2 shows all three climbing steeply with temperature.

The payoff is Figure 3: the visible emission of the seeded flame is essentially just these few resonance lines, sitting where the eye is most sensitive. Take the salt away and a clean premixed methane flame is very nearly invisible.

4Case B — the invisible flame: molecular IR

The flame’s products are hot CO₂ and H₂O — molecules with vibrational bands that radiate fiercely in the mid-infrared. HITRAN resolves each band into thousands of individual lines; in just the 2200–2400 cm⁻¹ window around the CO₂ 4.3 µm band the server reports 16,405 transitions, and 12,748 in the 600–750 cm⁻¹ window around the 15 µm bending region. Figure 4 plots the strongest sampled lines of the four dominant product bands.

Where the energy actually goes

How much of the flame’s radiation is visible at all? The Planck function sets the ceiling. At T_ad the black-body spectrum peaks (Wien’s law, λ = b/T) at 1.25 µm — deep in the near-infrared, invisible to the eye. Only 3.31% of a black body’s output at this temperature falls in the visible 380–750 nm window (about one part in 30), while 27.82% lies beyond 2.5 µm, exactly where the CO₂ and H₂O bands sit. Figure 5 overlays the two halves of the story on one axis.

PlanckBλ=2hc2λ5·1ehc/λkBT−1,λmax=b/T
A nuance worth stating plainly
A clean premixed flame is optically thin — it is not a black body. Its real emission is the sum of discrete molecular bands and atomic lines, well below the Planck ceiling, not a smooth continuum. So the visible thermal glow is fainter still than the few-percent ceiling suggests, and the orange you see is overwhelmingly the sodium line — an atomic-population effect — not hot-body glow. Meanwhile the flame’s genuine radiative loss is the CO₂ 4.3 µm band: precisely what an infrared flame detector or a rocket-plume sensor is built to see.

Confirming the fuel, from two more archives

Two independent measured spectra anchor the fuel’s identity. The NIST WebBook infrared spectrum shows methane’s ν₄ bending band at 1306 cm⁻¹; its electron-ionisation mass spectrum shows the molecular ion at m/z 16 with the CH₃⁺ fragment at 15. Faraday also reaches a second, independent mass-spectral archive — MoNA / MassBank — demonstrating that the same interface spans more than one repository.

5Limitations

The most credible part of a data pipeline is the set of checks it survives — and the honest list of what it cannot do.

Three self-audits the data passed

  • Constants vs. an independent implementation. Faraday’s CODATA values match SciPy’s CODATA-2022 set to a maximum relative difference of 3.50×10-10; the Stefan–Boltzmann constant assembled from Faraday’s k, h, c numerically matches SciPy’s value within the precision checked.
  • Thermochemistry vs. the textbook. The methane heating value derived purely from the NIST Shomate H-parameters, 802.30 kJ/mol, matches the canonical 802.3 kJ/mol to 2.62×10-6 — an end-to-end check that the enthalpy machinery is right.
  • One archive against itself. The ASD observed wavelengths (measured in air) and the wavelengths re-derived from the ASD energy levels (in vacuum) differ by exactly 0.163 nm at the Na D lines — the refractive index of air, n = 1.00028. The line list and the level table agree with each other through atmospheric dispersion.

Limitations, stated up front

  • The methanol gap. Faraday has no gas-phase Shomate for methanol; we pivoted to methane (§2). A genuine coverage gap, documented rather than papered over.
  • T_ad is an upper bound. The 2327 K figure assumes complete combustion and neglects the endothermic dissociation of CO₂/H₂O and radiative loss; a real stoichiometric methane–air flame runs a few hundred kelvin cooler. The Boltzmann and Planck conclusions are qualitatively robust across that range (Figure 2).
  • ASD is intermittent. Provenance must be re-checked every run — which is the point of the appended MCP call log, which lets you re-fetch and re-check every response on demand.
  • MoNA is fetch-by-ID only. Faraday exposes no MoNA discovery endpoint, so the record shown is a reference compound demonstrating the second archive is reachable; small gaseous fuels like methane are better served by NIST’s EI library, which we used for the fuel itself.
  • HITRAN lines are sampled. We capped each window at 40 lines to keep responses inline; the plotted intensities are representative and the quoted total line counts are exact, but the figures are not a full line-by-line band model.

6Outlook

The interesting thing is not the flame; it is that one agent answered a four-domain question by orchestrating six reference archives behind a single protocol, and left an audit trail in which every number is tied to the shipped data. The same pattern scales: a full line-by-line radiative-transfer model of a combustion plume, an opacity table for a stellar atmosphere, a reaction-network enthalpy budget — each is a cross-archive query that, today, a researcher assembles by hand from incompatible web front-ends.

Exposing legacy scientific infrastructure as typed, composable tools turns that assembly into something an agent can do in one pass — and, crucially, something it can verify. The deliverable of science is not just the answer; it is the chain from answer to source. Faraday is a small bet that the chain should be a first-class object.

Appendix C · Analysis code ▾

The calculation was produced by a SciPy workflow whose method is described here; the checked-in article package ships the call log plus the derived CSV and Parquet tables, so each number is traceable to a named Faraday tool call. No analysis scripts or raw-JSON bundles ship with the article. The raw HITRAN and NIST ASD line dumps are deliberately not redistributed here — those third-party datasets carry their own terms (see data/DATA_NOTICE.md) and are regenerated on demand through the same Faraday MCP calls; only the small derived and figure tables are shipped. The core numerical steps were:

# adiabatic flame temperature — NIST Shomate enthalpy balance
LHV  = -((dfH["CO2"] + 2*dfH["H2O"]) - (dfH["CH4"] + 2*0.0))      # 802.30 kJ/mol
sensible = lambda T: sum(nu[s]*H_minus_H298(shomate[s], T) for s in nu)
T_ad = brentq(lambda T: sensible(T) - LHV, 1000, 3500)            # 2327 K

# Boltzmann population of the Na 3p upper state at T_ad
theta = Ek_cm * c2_cm                                             # E_k/k_B  (c2 from CODATA)
frac  = sum(g_k*exp(-Ek*c2_cm/T_ad) for lines) / Z_na(T_ad)       # 8.3e-5

# Planck ceiling: Wien peak + visible fraction (CODATA only)
lam_peak = b_wien / T_ad                                          # 1.25 um
frac_vis = quad(lambda l: pi*planck(l,T_ad), 380e-9, 750e-9)[0] / (sigma*T_ad**4)
